What Does a Seismic Structural Engineer Do?

A seismic structural engineer applies structural mechanics, earthquake engineering principles, building-code requirements, and project-specific design criteria to determine how a structure should resist applicable seismic demands. The work goes considerably beyond simply calculating a single earthquake force. A complete engineering approach considers how seismic forces enter the building, travel through diaphragms and collectors, reach the lateral-force-resisting system, pass through structural connections, and ultimately transfer into foundations and supporting soil.

For a new building, a structural engineer for seismic design may participate in establishing the seismic design criteria, selecting an appropriate structural system, evaluating member capacities, checking stability, designing connections, and preparing engineering calculations and construction documents. Depending on the building, the lateral system could incorporate moment frames, braced frames, reinforced-concrete shear walls, masonry walls, or a combination of systems.

Seismic engineering also involves evaluating story drift and interstory drift, diaphragm behavior, structural irregularities, connection demands, anchorage, and foundation forces. These considerations can influence architectural layouts and MEP coordination, particularly in buildings with concentrated equipment or highly congested mechanical spaces.

For existing structures, a seismic assessment structural engineer may begin with available drawings and records before performing field verification. The investigation can identify changes made after original construction, deterioration, undocumented modifications, unusual structural conditions, or discrepancies between drawings and the current building.

A seismic retrofit structural engineer then develops strengthening concepts based on the identified deficiencies and the applicable evaluation methodology. Retrofit work might involve structural steel reinforcement, new shear walls, braced frames, connection strengthening, foundation modifications, or localized member upgrades.

The resulting engineering package can include calculations, structural drawings, specifications, details, permit documents, field observations, and construction coordination. The exact scope depends on the building, jurisdiction, structural system, and owner's objectives.

When Does a Building Need Seismic Structural Engineering?

Seismic structural engineering can become necessary at several stages of a building's life cycle. New construction is an obvious example, but seismic engineering is equally relevant when an existing structure is altered, expanded, evaluated, or repurposed. The precise requirements depend on the jurisdiction, adopted code edition, building characteristics, occupancy or Risk Category, site conditions, structural system, and project scope.

During new construction, seismic engineering is incorporated into the structural design from the beginning. The engineer establishes applicable seismic parameters and develops a lateral-force-resisting system capable of addressing the design demands. Early coordination is important because shear walls, braced frames, moment frames, collectors, foundations, and structural connections can influence architectural and MEP layouts.

Existing buildings present a different engineering challenge. An owner may seek a seismic engineering consultant near me because of an upcoming renovation, change in occupancy, addition, equipment installation, observed structural deficiency, or concern about the seismic performance of an older building. In these situations, the engineer must understand both the original structural design and the building's current condition.

Rooftop equipment is another common trigger for structural review. Adding chillers, air-handling equipment, generators, tanks, solar equipment, or other heavy components can introduce new gravity and seismic demands. The supporting framing, attachment points, roof structure, and load path may all require evaluation.

Industrial facilities can require specialized attention because equipment, platforms, piping, process systems, and structural steel often interact closely. Healthcare projects can introduce additional requirements associated with essential facilities and jurisdictional oversight.

A project may also require engineering because of an AHJ review, permit submission, insurance requirement, owner standard, or construction change. Consequently, searching for an earthquake structural engineer should not be limited to emergency situations. Early engineering involvement can identify structural constraints before they become costly design or construction conflicts.

Seismic Structural Design Under ASCE 7, IBC, and CBC

Seismic design must be based on the requirements governing the specific project rather than on a generic earthquake-resistance formula. In U.S. construction, the adopted building code and referenced standards establish the framework for determining applicable seismic criteria, structural demands, and design requirements.

ASCE 7 is a central reference for seismic design criteria in applicable building projects. Depending on the project, the engineer may evaluate parameters associated with site conditions, mapped seismic hazards, Risk Category, Seismic Design Category, response characteristics, and the selected structural system. These inputs influence the earthquake forces used in structural analysis and the resulting demands on members, connections, diaphragms, and foundations.

The IBC provides model-code provisions that jurisdictions adopt and modify through their own regulations. For California projects, the California Building Code forms part of the applicable regulatory framework, together with other portions of the California Building Standards Code and jurisdiction-specific amendments.

A California seismic structural engineer therefore needs to account for the edition of the CBC adopted by the authority having jurisdiction rather than assuming that every California project follows identical requirements. Healthcare facilities under the jurisdiction of the California Department of Health Care Access and Information, commonly known as HCAI and formerly associated with OSHPD, may have additional requirements and review procedures.

Important engineering concepts include base shear, lateral force distribution, diaphragm behavior, story drift, interstory drift, load combinations, and the response of the selected lateral-force-resisting system. The engineer also has to consider how forces transfer through structural connections and into foundations.

For existing buildings, ASCE 41 may provide an applicable framework for seismic evaluation and rehabilitation, depending on the project's objectives and governing requirements. FEMA guidance can also provide valuable technical information, although individual projects remain governed by their applicable codes, standards, specifications, and AHJ requirements.

Seismic Analysis and Structural Calculations

A seismic analysis transforms project information into quantifiable structural demands. The process begins with understanding the building geometry, structural materials, framing system, occupancy, site conditions, applicable seismic criteria, and expected load path. From there, the engineer develops calculations and analytical models appropriate to the structural system and project scope.

Structural Load Path

A fundamental concept is the continuous seismic load path:

Diaphragm → Collectors → Frames or Walls → Foundations → Soil

Earthquake-induced forces must be transferred through connected structural elements without relying on unverified assumptions. Floor and roof diaphragms may collect and distribute lateral forces to shear walls or frames. Collectors transfer forces into the vertical lateral system, while connections transfer forces between individual members. Foundations then deliver the resulting demands to the supporting soil.

Structural Analysis

A seismic analysis may evaluate lateral forces, member forces, structural response, stability, connection demands, diaphragm behavior, and drift. Depending on the building and analytical approach, engineers may use simplified procedures or more advanced modeling methods. The appropriate method depends on the project's characteristics and governing requirements.

Seismic Calculations

Professional seismic calculations should correspond to the actual structure rather than being generic documents detached from the building configuration. Calculations can document seismic criteria, assumptions, load combinations, structural demands, member checks, connection requirements, and other relevant engineering decisions.

For an owner or contractor, clear calculations are also important during permit review and construction. They provide a technical record showing how the design criteria were translated into structural requirements.

Seismic Assessment and Retrofit of Existing Buildings

Existing-building seismic engineering requires a different approach from designing a structure entirely from new materials and drawings. The engineer must establish what actually exists before determining what needs to change. Original structural drawings, previous renovation documents, field observations, material information, and available maintenance records can all contribute to the assessment.

Field verification is particularly important when drawings are incomplete or the building has undergone multiple modifications. A seismic assessment structural engineer may investigate structural framing, connections, diaphragms, foundations, walls, columns, beams, braces, and other components that contribute to seismic resistance. Deterioration, corrosion, cracking, undocumented openings, removed walls, modified connections, and added loads can materially affect the analysis.

The assessment then identifies potential deficiencies in the structural load path or individual components. Depending on the project, the engineer may evaluate lateral capacity, drift, connection performance, anchorage, foundation demands, and compatibility between existing construction and proposed modifications.

For projects involving rehabilitation of existing buildings, ASCE 41 can provide an applicable framework for seismic evaluation and retrofit. The appropriate methodology depends on the building, evaluation objective, jurisdiction, and project requirements.

Retrofit strategies must be developed specifically for the structure. Possible approaches include strengthening existing shear walls, introducing steel braced frames, modifying moment-frame components, strengthening connections, reinforcing individual members, or improving foundations. In some cases, localized strengthening may address a deficiency without requiring a major change to the overall structural system.

Successful retrofit design also considers construction sequencing and building use. A hospital, manufacturing facility, occupied office building, or data center may have very different constraints. New reinforcement may need to avoid MEP systems, architectural finishes, equipment, access routes, and operational areas.

The objective is not simply to add steel or concrete. It is to develop a technically justified strengthening strategy that improves the relevant structural behavior while remaining compatible with the existing building and construction conditions.

Structural Systems, Connections, and Anchorage Evaluated by a Seismic Engineer

A seismic structural engineer may work with a wide range of structural systems, including steel framing, reinforced concrete, prestressed concrete, structural masonry, CMU, and mixed-material construction. Each system transfers earthquake forces differently, so the analytical and detailing requirements must reflect its actual behavior.

Moment Frames

Moment frames resist lateral forces through rigid or partially rigid beam-column connections and the flexural behavior of structural members. Depending on the design, the system may include ordinary or special moment frames with different detailing and performance requirements.

Braced Frames

Braced frames use diagonal structural members to provide lateral resistance. Depending on the design, systems may include special concentrically braced frames, eccentrically braced frames, or other configurations. Brace connections, gusset plates, beam-column interactions, and force transfer are important parts of the design.

Shear Walls

Reinforced-concrete and masonry shear walls can provide significant lateral resistance. Engineers may need to evaluate wall strength, boundary elements, reinforcement, collectors, diaphragm connections, foundations, and overturning effects.

Foundations and Anchorage

The seismic load path ultimately reaches the foundation system. Footings, foundation walls, structural slabs, anchor rods, base plates, and soil interfaces may therefore require evaluation. Anchorage can be particularly important where large overturning or shear forces develop.

Connection engineering can involve structural steel plates, angles, channels, HSS members, high-strength bolts, welds, anchor rods, cast-in anchors, and post-installed anchoring systems. AISC 360 and ACI 318 may be relevant depending on the material and application, while project-specific specifications and manufacturer requirements must also be considered.

The engineering challenge is continuity. A strong beam or brace cannot compensate for an inadequate connection, and a properly designed connection cannot transfer forces into an insufficient foundation. Seismic structural engineering therefore evaluates the system as a connected load path rather than as isolated components.

Seismic Engineering for MEP Systems and Equipment

Seismic performance does not end with the primary structural frame. Mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems can contain substantial equipment and distribution infrastructure that requires appropriate seismic support or anchorage where applicable. This makes coordination between structural engineering and MEP disciplines especially important.

Mechanical systems may include air-handling units, chillers, boilers, pumps, fans, cooling equipment, and extensive piping and ductwork. Electrical systems can include generators, transformers, switchgear, conduit, cable trays, and emergency-power equipment. Plumbing and specialty systems may include water piping, fire protection, medical gas, and other critical services.

ASCE 7 Chapter 13 addresses applicable nonstructural components and their supports and attachments. The exact requirements depend on the component, building, seismic criteria, and project conditions. A seismic structural engineer may therefore need to coordinate equipment anchorage, support framing, attachment points, and structural capacity with the broader building design.

MEP seismic bracing can include trapezes, strut channel assemblies, pipe supports, duct bracing, conduit bracing, cable-tray bracing, and equipment restraints. The supporting structure must be capable of accepting the resulting forces, and the attachment between the restraint and structure must form a reliable load path.

This work is distinct from primary structural seismic design, but the two disciplines often intersect. For example, an HVAC support may impose concentrated forces on a roof structure, while an equipment anchorage system may require structural verification of the concrete or steel supporting element.

Frequently Asked Questions About Seismic Structural Engineers

What does a seismic structural engineer near me do?

A seismic structural engineer evaluates and designs structures to address applicable earthquake demands. The scope can include seismic design criteria, structural analysis, lateral-force-resisting systems, member design, connections, foundations, anchorage, drift evaluation, and construction documentation. For existing buildings, the work may include field verification, seismic assessment, deficiency identification, and retrofit design. For projects involving MEP infrastructure, the engineer may also coordinate structural capacity and attachments for applicable seismic supports and equipment anchorage.

How much does seismic structural engineering cost?

There is no single fee for seismic structural engineering because project scope varies significantly. Cost can depend on building size, structural system, location, documentation quality, existing-condition complexity, analytical requirements, permit requirements, retrofit scope, field investigation, and construction coordination. A new commercial building, a limited equipment-anchorage review, and a comprehensive seismic assessment of an existing industrial facility represent very different engineering scopes. A meaningful proposal should therefore define deliverables, assumptions, site visits, calculations, drawings, coordination, and revisions rather than relying only on building size.

When is a seismic structural assessment required?

A seismic assessment may be appropriate when an existing building is being evaluated for earthquake performance, modified, expanded, repurposed, or considered for rehabilitation. It can also become relevant when an owner has concerns about structural deficiencies, undocumented modifications, deterioration, or changes that affect the existing load path. The exact legal or code requirement depends on the jurisdiction, adopted code, building characteristics, project scope, and applicable regulations.

What is included in seismic structural calculations?

Seismic calculations may document the applicable seismic criteria, structural assumptions, loads, load combinations, analytical approach, member forces, drift, stability, connection demands, and other project-specific checks. The exact contents depend on the structural system and scope. Calculations should correspond to the actual building rather than being generic templates. They may support permit documents, design review, construction coordination, or evaluation of an existing structure.

Does ASCE 7 apply to seismic structural design?

ASCE 7 is a major reference for seismic design criteria used in applicable U.S. building design. It addresses topics relevant to determining earthquake demands and structural design parameters. However, the enforceable requirements for a project come from the applicable adopted building code and jurisdictional requirements, which may incorporate ASCE 7 by reference with amendments or additional provisions. Engineers should therefore establish the governing code edition and project requirements before performing final design.

What is the difference between ASCE 7 and ASCE 41?

ASCE 7 is widely used for structural design loads and seismic design criteria for applicable new construction and other situations covered by the governing code. ASCE 41 provides a framework for seismic evaluation and rehabilitation of existing buildings. They address different engineering objectives, although an existing-building project can involve multiple standards and regulatory requirements. The applicable methodology should be selected based on the project's scope, jurisdiction, building characteristics, and evaluation objective.

Can a structural engineer design seismic retrofits for existing buildings?

Yes, seismic retrofit engineering is an important part of structural engineering. Retrofit design begins with understanding the existing structure, including its framing, materials, connections, foundations, modifications, and current condition. The engineer then identifies relevant deficiencies and develops strengthening measures appropriate to the building. Possible solutions include shear-wall strengthening, braced frames, moment-frame modifications, connection strengthening, member reinforcement, and foundation improvements. No single retrofit method is appropriate for every building.

Does a California building require CBC seismic engineering?

California buildings are subject to the applicable provisions of the California Building Code and other portions of the California Building Standards Code, along with local jurisdictional requirements. The exact seismic engineering requirements depend on the building, location, occupancy, structural system, adopted code edition, and project scope. California projects should therefore be evaluated against the requirements applicable to their specific jurisdiction rather than assuming that one seismic design approach applies statewide.

When do HCAI or OSHPD requirements apply?

HCAI, formerly associated with the Office of Statewide Health Planning and Development or OSHPD, has jurisdiction over applicable California healthcare facilities. Hospitals and other covered healthcare projects can have specialized structural, seismic, review, and documentation requirements. HCAI requirements should not be treated as universal requirements for ordinary commercial or industrial buildings. The project type and jurisdiction determine whether HCAI oversight applies.

Can a seismic structural engineer design equipment anchorage and MEP supports?

A structural engineer can evaluate structural capacity and design or coordinate applicable anchorage and support systems, depending on the project's scope and professional responsibilities. Equipment anchorage may require evaluation of the supported equipment, supporting structure, anchors, connections, and seismic forces. MEP seismic bracing may involve ductwork, piping, conduit, cable trays, trapezes, and equipment supports. Effective coordination is important because these systems must connect to the building structure through a compatible load path.

Can seismic engineering be coordinated through BIM and 3D CAD?

Yes. BIM and 3D CAD can support structural modeling, MEP coordination, clash detection, attachment-location review, equipment-support layouts, seismic bracing coordination, and fabrication documentation. Digital coordination is especially useful in mechanical rooms, rooftops, central utility plants, hospitals, data centers, and industrial facilities where structural and MEP systems compete for limited space. BIM does not replace engineering judgment or calculations, but it can improve communication between design and construction teams.

What should I ask a seismic structural engineering firm before hiring it?

Useful questions include whether the firm has experience with the project's building type, seismic conditions, jurisdiction, and structural system; whether it performs seismic calculations and existing-building assessments; whether it has retrofit experience; and whether it can coordinate with architects, MEP engineers, contractors, and the AHJ. For complex projects, it can also be useful to ask about BIM, field verification, equipment anchorage, MEP seismic bracing, construction administration, and the firm's ability to coordinate custom structural components with fabrication requirements.
