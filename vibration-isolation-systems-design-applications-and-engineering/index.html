Vibration Isolation: Systems, Design, Applications, and Engineering

anitaa October 1, 2026

Vibration isolation is an engineered method of reducing the transmission of mechanical vibration from equipment into its supporting structure and, in some applications, limiting vibration transmitted from the structure into sensitive equipment. It is used across commercial buildings, healthcare facilities, industrial plants, manufacturing environments, marine systems, and other facilities where mechanical vibration can affect equipment performance, structural behavior, occupant comfort, acoustic conditions, or adjacent operations.

A vibration isolation system is more than a spring, rubber mount, or isolation pad installed beneath a machine. Effective performance depends on the relationship between equipment mass, operating speed, excitation frequency, isolator stiffness, static deflection, natural frequency, damping, mounting geometry, and structural support conditions. The selection process also has to account for environmental exposure, installation constraints, maintenance requirements, and, where applicable, seismic restraint.

For engineers and contractors, this distinction is important because an isolator selected only according to equipment weight may not provide the intended level of vibration control. A pump, fan, chiller, compressor, generator, or rotating machine produces dynamic forces that vary with operating conditions. The isolation system has to respond appropriately to those forces while keeping the equipment stable and maintaining a reliable load path into the supporting structure.

Vibration isolation also intersects with—but should not be confused with—vibration damping, acoustic isolation, and seismic protection. Each addresses a different engineering problem, although a complete equipment-support assembly may incorporate several of these functions.

For U.S. construction projects, vibration isolation must also be coordinated with the structural and mechanical design of the building. On California projects, engineers may need to consider the California Building Code (CBC), project-specific HCAI requirements, and seismic design provisions associated with ASCE 7 and the International Building Code (IBC).

The result is a multidisciplinary design problem involving mechanical equipment, structural supports, isolator characteristics, anchorage, fabrication, and installation. Understanding those relationships provides the foundation for selecting a vibration isolation system that is appropriate for the actual application.

What Is Vibration Isolation?

Vibration isolation is the controlled reduction of vibratory force transmission between a vibrating source and the structure or equipment connected to it. In a typical installation, the basic mechanical relationship can be represented as:

Vibrating equipment → isolation system → supporting structure

The equipment generates excitation forces as a result of rotating, reciprocating, pulsating, or other mechanical activity. Without an appropriate isolation interface, some of that energy can travel directly into the floor, roof, support frame, foundation, or connected building components.

The purpose of an isolator is to alter that transmission path. Instead of creating a rigid connection between equipment and structure, the isolation system introduces a controlled degree of compliance. Depending on the design, that compliance may be provided by steel springs, elastomeric materials, wire rope, or other engineered components.

Vibration Isolation vs. Vibration Damping

Isolation and damping are related but different concepts. Vibration isolation primarily seeks to reduce the force or motion transmitted between a source and its support. Damping dissipates vibratory energy and can reduce response near resonance. An isolation system may incorporate damping characteristics, but damping alone does not automatically create effective isolation.

This distinction becomes especially important at low frequencies. An isolator that appears soft or highly compliant may still perform poorly if its dynamic characteristics are not appropriate for the equipment's operating frequency.

Vibration Isolation vs. Seismic Isolation

Operational vibration isolation should also be distinguished from seismic isolation. Vibration isolation is generally concerned with mechanical excitation generated during normal equipment operation. Seismic design addresses forces and movements associated with earthquakes.

A mechanical unit can therefore require vibration isolation while also requiring seismic restraint or anchorage. The two requirements need to be coordinated rather than treated as interchangeable.

For example, an HVAC unit may require flexible isolation to reduce operational vibration while simultaneously requiring restraints capable of addressing seismic movement. The isolation system, restraint hardware, equipment support, and structural attachment all have to work together without unintentionally compromising the intended vibration-control performance.

How Does a Vibration Isolation System Work?

The fundamental behavior of a vibration isolation system can be understood through the relationship between mass, stiffness, damping, and frequency. A piece of equipment supported on an isolator behaves differently from equipment rigidly attached to a structure because the isolator introduces a mechanical degree of compliance.

One of the most important parameters is natural frequency. The natural frequency represents the frequency at which the equipment-isolator system tends to oscillate when disturbed. For a simplified single-degree-of-freedom system, natural frequency is related to effective stiffness and supported mass.

Static deflection is closely related to this behavior. When equipment is placed on a spring or other compliant isolator, its weight produces a static displacement. The stiffness of the isolator and the supported mass determine the resulting dynamic characteristics.

Frequency Ratio and Transmissibility

The relationship between operating frequency and isolation-system natural frequency is central to isolation performance. Engineers often evaluate the frequency ratio, which compares excitation frequency with system natural frequency.

When the operating frequency is sufficiently separated from the isolation system's natural frequency, the system can enter a region where transmitted vibration is significantly reduced. Near resonance, however, vibration response can increase rather than decrease.

This is why simply specifying a "soft" mount does not guarantee good isolation. The mount must have appropriate stiffness and load capacity for the equipment and must be evaluated against the actual operating conditions.

Static Deflection and Natural Frequency

For spring-based systems, greater static deflection generally corresponds to lower natural frequency. That characteristic can be valuable when isolation of low-frequency equipment is required. However, the design still has to maintain equipment stability and accommodate the actual load distribution.

Variable-speed equipment introduces additional considerations because the operating frequency can change. Startup and shutdown conditions can also pass through frequency ranges where the system behaves differently from its steady-state operating condition.

A technically sound vibration isolation design therefore considers the entire operating range rather than a single nominal RPM.

Types of Vibration Isolation Systems

Different equipment and environments require different isolation mechanisms. No single vibration isolator is appropriate for every application. Selection should account for loading, frequency, movement, environmental exposure, installation configuration, and required performance.

Spring Vibration Isolators

Spring vibration isolators are widely used for mechanical equipment requiring substantial static deflection and low-frequency isolation. Applications can include air-handling units, pumps, fans, chillers, cooling towers, generators, and other heavy equipment.

Steel springs can provide relatively low natural frequencies when properly selected, making them useful for equipment where significant vibration reduction is required. Restrained or captive configurations may also be used where movement limits or additional restraint are necessary.

Spring selection should consider operating load at each mounting location rather than simply dividing total equipment weight equally among mounts. Equipment center of gravity, mounting geometry, and uneven load distribution can materially affect individual spring loads.

Rubber and Elastomeric Isolators

Rubber and elastomeric isolators can provide compact support while combining compliance with material damping. Depending on the compound and geometry, elastomeric mounts can be appropriate for equipment with moderate loads, limited installation space, or applications where compact mounting is advantageous.

Material selection matters. Natural rubber, synthetic rubber, neoprene, EPDM, and other elastomeric compounds can behave differently under temperature, moisture, oils, chemicals, and long-term loading.

Wire Rope Vibration Isolators

Wire rope isolators use helically wound metal cable to provide compliance and damping characteristics. Their construction can make them useful in applications involving shock, multidirectional motion, harsh environments, or specialized equipment.

Marine, aerospace, industrial, and mobile equipment can present conditions that differ substantially from those found in a conventional mechanical room. Wire rope systems can therefore provide an alternative where environmental durability and multidirectional response are important considerations.

Acoustic Hangers and Floor Isolation

Suspended HVAC and MEP equipment may require vibration isolation hangers to limit transmission through structural connections. Floor-mounted equipment can use isolation pads or dedicated floor vibration isolators.

The correct configuration depends on whether the dominant transmission path is through a floor, frame, ceiling support, pipe connection, duct connection, or another structural interface.

How to Select the Right Vibration Isolator

Vibration isolator selection should begin with the equipment and its operating conditions rather than with a preferred product type. A spring, elastomeric mount, or wire rope isolator should be selected because its mechanical characteristics match the application.

The first requirement is accurate equipment information. Engineers should establish operating weight, mounting-point locations, center of gravity, dimensions, operating speed, and expected dynamic loads.

Load Distribution

Total equipment weight is not enough by itself. Each isolator must be capable of carrying its actual operating load. Uneven equipment geometry can cause some mounts to carry substantially more load than others.

The equipment's center of gravity is particularly important for long or irregular machines. A theoretically equal number of identical mounts does not necessarily mean equal loading.

Operating Frequency

Operating RPM should be converted into the relevant excitation frequency, while harmonics and other excitation sources should also be considered where applicable. Variable-speed equipment requires evaluation across its operating range.

A fan operating at one fixed speed presents a different isolation-selection problem from a variable-speed motor that continuously changes frequency.

Static Deflection

Static deflection provides an important indication of an isolator's stiffness under load. For spring systems, it is directly related to the system's natural frequency.

However, static deflection should not be treated as an isolated specification. Engineers must consider whether the resulting system provides adequate equipment stability, movement control, clearance, and compatibility with the structure.

Environment and Installation

Temperature, moisture, oil, fuel, chemicals, UV exposure, corrosion, and marine conditions can affect material selection and service life. Installation space and mounting geometry can also eliminate otherwise suitable components.

A technically appropriate isolator must therefore satisfy both dynamic requirements and physical installation constraints.

Vibration Isolation for HVAC and MEP Equipment

HVAC and MEP systems are among the most common applications for vibration isolation because mechanical equipment can transmit vibration through building structures and connected services. Equipment such as air-handling units, pumps, fans, chillers, compressors, cooling towers, and generators can produce different excitation characteristics and therefore require application-specific evaluation.

Air-handling units, for example, may be installed on structural floors, housekeeping pads, roof structures, or fabricated support frames. The isolation system has to accommodate equipment weight while maintaining a stable mounting arrangement.

Pumps and fans introduce rotating forces that can vary with operating speed. Poorly selected mounts can allow excessive movement, while overly stiff mounts may transmit more vibration into the structure than intended. The connection of piping and other services must also be considered because rigid connected systems can create alternate vibration transmission paths.

Chillers and cooling towers can impose substantial loads on supporting structures. Their vibration isolation design may need to be coordinated with equipment bases, inertia bases, housekeeping pads, structural steel, and seismic restraint.

Suspended equipment presents a different challenge. Acoustic hangers and vibration isolation hangers can decouple equipment or services from overhead structural supports, but the complete suspension assembly must remain structurally adequate.

The same principle applies to MEP coordination: vibration isolation cannot be considered independently from the surrounding system. Flexible connections, piping supports, ductwork, electrical connections, equipment anchors, and structural attachments can all influence the final vibration path.

For contractors and specification writers, this means that an equipment schedule should provide enough information to allow proper isolator selection rather than simply calling for a generic "vibration isolation mount."

Vibration Isolation for Industrial and Rotating Equipment

Industrial equipment often creates more demanding vibration-control conditions than conventional commercial HVAC systems. Motors, compressors, pumps, engines, generators, production machinery, and other rotating equipment can generate significant dynamic forces across a range of operating frequencies.

A major consideration is the relationship between equipment excitation and the isolation system's dynamic response. Machinery with substantial rotating mass may require isolation that maintains stability while reducing force transmission.

Generators and engines can also create low-frequency and reciprocating forces. Their isolation design may incorporate rubber/metal mounts, captive isolators, spring systems, inertia bases, or other specialized configurations depending on the equipment and installation.

Harsh environments introduce another layer of engineering requirements. Marine and mobile equipment may encounter oil, diesel fuel, moisture, salt exposure, shock, and multidirectional movement. Materials and hardware therefore need to be selected for the actual operating environment rather than assuming indoor building conditions.

Wire rope isolators can be useful in applications where multidirectional response and environmental durability are important. Rubber/metal systems can provide compact mounting arrangements for engines and rotating equipment when their material and stiffness characteristics are appropriate.

Industrial vibration isolation also has to consider maintenance. An isolator that is difficult to inspect, replace, or access may create operational problems even if its initial dynamic characteristics are appropriate.

For procurement professionals, the critical question is not simply "Which mount fits this machine?" but rather "Which isolation assembly provides the required mechanical behavior under the actual operating and environmental conditions?"

Vibration Isolation Design and Structural Engineering

Vibration isolation is inseparable from the structure supporting the equipment. An isolator can have appropriate rated capacity and still perform differently in service if the supporting floor, frame, base, or foundation is insufficiently stiff or configured incorrectly.

Equipment foundations and inertia bases are often used to provide a stable support platform. An inertia base can increase the effective mass of the supported system and help address equipment stability and dynamic behavior. Its use should be determined by the equipment and isolation design rather than assumed for every application.

Structural floors and rooftop supports also require evaluation. Heavy equipment can create concentrated loads, while dynamic forces may influence the design of supporting members and connections. The isolator's static deflection must be compatible with available clearances and the surrounding structure.

Fabricated support frames introduce additional considerations. Steel members, brackets, base plates, welds, anchors, and connections become part of the load path. The vibration isolation system therefore needs to be coordinated with the fabricated assembly rather than treated as an independent component.

BIM and 3D CAD coordination can help identify conflicts before fabrication. Equipment dimensions, mounting points, isolator locations, structural members, access clearances, and service connections can be reviewed as a coordinated assembly.

This engineering approach is particularly valuable when standard catalog geometry does not match actual project conditions. A custom mounting frame or fabricated base can bridge the gap between the equipment manufacturer's mounting arrangement and the building's structural support.

The objective is not merely to support the equipment. It is to establish a predictable mechanical load path while maintaining the intended isolation characteristics.

Vibration Isolation, Seismic Restraint, and Building Codes

Vibration isolation and seismic protection often intersect on U.S. commercial, healthcare, and industrial projects, but they address different design conditions.

Operational vibration comes from the equipment itself. Seismic loading results from earthquake-induced movement of the building and its components. An isolation system intended to control normal operating vibration may not by itself provide the restraint required for seismic loading.

ASCE 7 provides seismic design provisions that can be relevant to nonstructural components and equipment support. The IBC establishes a broader building-code framework, while the CBC governs applicable California projects. The actual design requirements depend on the project location, occupancy, equipment classification, structural system, and applicable code edition.

Healthcare facilities require additional coordination. Projects under the jurisdiction of California's Department of Health Care Access and Information (HCAI), formerly associated with OSHPD terminology, can have project-specific documentation, review, and compliance requirements.

The key engineering principle is that seismic restraint should not unintentionally defeat the vibration-isolation function. Restraints, snubbers, anchors, flexible connections, and clearances may need to accommodate both operating movement and seismic movement.

A project should therefore distinguish between:

  • Operational vibration isolation

  • Seismic restraint

  • Equipment anchorage

  • Structural support

  • Flexible utility connections

Where a specific vibration isolation assembly is required to satisfy seismic criteria, the applicable calculations, testing, listings, pre-approval status, or project documentation should be verified rather than assumed.

For California healthcare work, this distinction is especially important. HCAI/OSHPD-related terminology should be used accurately, and project-specific requirements should be confirmed by the responsible design professionals and authorities having jurisdiction.

When Standard Vibration Isolators Are Not Enough

Standard isolators work well when equipment loads, geometry, operating conditions, and environmental exposure fall within their intended design range. Custom vibration isolation becomes relevant when those conditions do not align with a conventional component.

Examples include equipment with unusual weight distribution, nonstandard mounting footprints, high dynamic loads, limited installation space, unusual operating frequencies, or harsh marine and industrial environments.

A custom system may involve more than modifying the isolator itself. The complete assembly can include custom spring configurations, wire rope assemblies, elastomeric mounts, rubber/metal components, mounting brackets, structural frames, inertia bases, rails, restraints, and custom strut channels.

The engineering process should begin by defining the equipment requirements. Weight, center of gravity, mounting locations, operating speed, excitation forces, environmental conditions, available clearances, and structural support conditions provide the basis for determining whether a standard component is appropriate.

Custom fabrication can then translate the engineering requirements into a physical assembly. Stainless steel may be appropriate where corrosion resistance is important; carbon or structural steel may provide economical structural support; aluminum may be considered where weight reduction is a priority. Galvanizing or powder coating can provide additional protection where compatible with the service environment.

How to Specify a Vibration Isolation System

A useful vibration isolation specification should provide enough information for an engineer or qualified supplier to evaluate the application accurately.

Equipment Information

The equipment schedule should identify the equipment type, manufacturer and model where available, operating weight, dimensions, mounting locations, center of gravity, and operating condition. If equipment weight changes significantly between empty, operating, and maximum conditions, those cases should be identified.

Dynamic Information

Operating RPM, excitation frequency, harmonics, startup and shutdown conditions, and known dynamic forces should be provided when available. Variable-speed equipment should be evaluated across its relevant operating range.

Installation Information

The design should identify whether equipment is floor-mounted, suspended, rooftop-mounted, mounted on structural steel, or supported by an inertia base. Available footprint, clearance, support stiffness, anchor locations, and surrounding construction should also be considered.

Environmental and Seismic Information

Temperature, moisture, oils, fuels, chemicals, outdoor exposure, marine conditions, and corrosion risks can affect material selection. Project-specific seismic requirements should be identified separately from normal vibration-control requirements.

Documentation

Depending on the project, useful documentation can include product data, load-deflection information, natural-frequency data, engineering calculations, CAD/BIM models, installation instructions, seismic documentation, and compliance information.

Providing these inputs early reduces the risk of selecting an isolator that is technically unsuitable or physically incompatible with the installation.

For specification writers and procurement teams, the goal should be performance-based clarity rather than vague language such as "provide vibration isolation." The specification should establish the equipment conditions and required engineering information while allowing the appropriate isolation system to be selected.

Conclusion

Vibration isolation is fundamentally a system-design problem. Effective isolation depends on understanding how equipment generates excitation, how that excitation interacts with an isolator, and how the resulting forces are transmitted into the supporting structure. Equipment weight is only one part of the selection process.

Engineers must also consider operating frequency, RPM, static deflection, stiffness, natural frequency, damping, transmissibility, load distribution, center of gravity, mounting geometry, environmental exposure, and structural support conditions. For industrial and marine equipment, shock and environmental durability may become particularly important. For HVAC and MEP systems, the isolation system must be coordinated with piping, ductwork, supports, and building structure.

Spring vibration isolators, rubber and elastomeric mounts, wire rope isolators, acoustic hangers, and floor vibration isolators each serve different engineering purposes. The appropriate selection depends on the actual application rather than on a universal preference for one technology.

Seismic requirements add another layer. Vibration isolation should not be confused with seismic restraint, and projects subject to ASCE 7, IBC, CBC, or California healthcare requirements may require coordinated evaluation of isolation, anchorage, restraint, and structural support. HCAI/OSHPD-related requirements should be addressed according to the specific project and applicable authority.

When standard components do not accommodate equipment loading, geometry, environmental exposure, or project constraints, custom vibration isolation can combine engineered isolators with fabricated frames, bases, brackets, and support assemblies. BIM and CAD coordination can further connect the design to the surrounding structure and MEP systems.

For engineers, contractors, facility managers, and procurement teams, the most useful starting point is a complete description of the equipment and operating environment. With accurate load, frequency, geometry, structural, environmental, and seismic information, vibration isolation can be evaluated as an integrated engineering system rather than a standalone mounting component.

Frequently Asked Questions About Vibration Isolation

What is vibration isolation?

Vibration isolation is the controlled reduction of vibration transmitted between equipment and its supporting structure. An isolation system introduces a mechanical interface with specific stiffness and dynamic characteristics between the vibration source and its support.

The goal is not necessarily to eliminate vibration at the source. Instead, the isolation system changes the force and motion transmission path. Depending on the application, the system may use springs, elastomers, wire rope, acoustic hangers, isolation pads, or other engineered components.

Effective performance depends on factors such as equipment mass, operating frequency, isolator stiffness, natural frequency, damping, static deflection, and installation conditions.

How does vibration isolation work?

A vibration isolation system changes the mechanical connection between equipment and its support. The equipment and isolator form a dynamic system with a natural frequency determined largely by supported mass and effective stiffness.

When the equipment's excitation frequency is sufficiently separated from the isolation system's natural frequency, vibration transmission can be reduced. Near resonance, however, response can increase significantly.

For this reason, selecting an isolator based only on its load rating is not enough. Engineers need to evaluate the equipment's operating conditions and the dynamic characteristics of the complete isolation system.

What is the difference between vibration isolation and vibration damping?

Vibration isolation and damping address different aspects of mechanical vibration.

Isolation reduces transmission between a vibration source and its support by introducing compliance into the mechanical path. Damping dissipates vibratory energy and can reduce the magnitude of response, particularly around resonance.

A vibration isolation system can have both isolation and damping characteristics. Rubber and elastomeric materials, for example, can provide material damping while also creating a compliant connection. Steel spring systems may provide strong low-frequency isolation but often require separate consideration of damping characteristics.

The correct approach depends on the equipment and performance objective.

What type of vibration isolator is best for HVAC equipment?

There is no single best vibration isolator for every HVAC application. Selection depends on equipment weight, operating speed, excitation frequency, mounting arrangement, required static deflection, structural support, available space, and project requirements.

Spring isolators are commonly considered for heavy HVAC equipment where substantial static deflection and low-frequency isolation are important. Elastomeric mounts can be useful where compact support and material damping are appropriate. Acoustic hangers can be used for suspended systems.

Chillers, air-handling units, pumps, fans, cooling towers, and compressors can have substantially different requirements, so the isolator should be selected based on the actual equipment data rather than equipment category alone.

Are spring isolators better than rubber vibration isolators?

Spring and rubber isolators have different mechanical characteristics, so neither should be considered universally superior.

Spring systems can provide substantial static deflection and relatively low natural frequencies, making them useful for certain heavy or low-frequency applications. Rubber and elastomeric systems can offer compact configurations and material damping, with performance dependent on compound, geometry, temperature, loading, and environmental conditions.

The engineering comparison should consider load range, stiffness, required deflection, operating frequency, equipment stability, footprint, environmental exposure, and maintenance requirements.

When should wire rope vibration isolators be used?

Wire rope vibration isolators can be considered for applications involving shock, multidirectional motion, harsh environments, and specialized industrial, marine, aerospace, or mobile equipment.

Their metal construction can provide useful environmental characteristics in situations where conventional elastomeric components may face challenging exposure conditions. Their suitability still depends on load, geometry, required dynamic response, and the specific application.

Wire rope should therefore be selected based on actual performance requirements rather than simply because the equipment operates in a harsh environment.

What is static deflection in a vibration isolation system?

Static deflection is the displacement produced when the supported equipment load is applied to an isolator.

For spring-based systems, static deflection is closely related to stiffness and natural frequency. In general, a softer spring supporting a given mass produces greater deflection and a lower natural frequency.

Static deflection is therefore an important selection parameter, but it should not be considered by itself. Equipment stability, clearance, load distribution, operating frequency, and structural conditions also have to be evaluated.

How is natural frequency used when selecting vibration isolators?

Natural frequency helps engineers determine how an isolation system will respond to equipment excitation.

The operating frequency of the equipment is compared with the natural frequency of the equipment-isolator system. This frequency ratio influences transmissibility and determines whether the system operates near resonance or in a region where isolation can be effective.

This is why information such as motor RPM, fan speed, pump speed, excitation frequency, and variable-speed operating range is important when selecting an isolator.

Can vibration isolation systems also provide seismic protection?

A vibration isolation system and a seismic restraint system have different functions.

Vibration isolation primarily addresses mechanical vibration generated during equipment operation. Seismic protection addresses earthquake-induced forces and movement. Depending on the project, equipment may need both.

Seismic restraints, anchors, snubbers, or other provisions may need to be coordinated with the isolation system so that they provide the required seismic resistance without unnecessarily restricting normal operating movement.

Projects subject to ASCE 7, IBC, CBC, or California healthcare requirements should be evaluated according to the applicable project criteria rather than assuming that a standard vibration isolator automatically satisfies seismic requirements.

Does ASCE 7 apply to vibration isolation systems?

ASCE 7 is primarily a structural loading standard, including provisions relevant to seismic and other environmental loading. It is not a general vibration-isolation performance standard.

ASCE 7 can become relevant when vibration-isolated equipment is also subject to seismic design requirements, particularly for nonstructural components and equipment support or restraint. The applicable provisions depend on the project and component being designed.

For California projects, ASCE 7 requirements are coordinated with the applicable building-code framework, including the CBC and project-specific requirements. Healthcare projects under HCAI jurisdiction may have additional requirements.

When does a project require custom vibration isolators?

Custom vibration isolation may be appropriate when standard components cannot adequately accommodate the equipment's load, geometry, operating conditions, environment, or project constraints.

Typical circumstances can include unusual weight distribution, nonstandard mounting footprints, high dynamic loads, limited installation space, specialized marine or industrial environments, retrofit conditions, or project-specific seismic and structural requirements.

Custom engineering may involve the isolator itself as well as fabricated bases, frames, brackets, rails, inertia bases, restraints, and other structural components.

What information should I provide when requesting a vibration isolation design?

A useful engineering review should begin with accurate equipment information.

Provide the equipment type and model when available, operating weight, dimensions, mounting locations, center of gravity, operating RPM, excitation information, variable-speed range, and known dynamic loads.

The installation should also be described, including floor or suspended mounting, structural support conditions, available footprint, anchor locations, clearances, and surrounding construction.

Environmental conditions such as temperature, moisture, oil, fuel, chemicals, outdoor exposure, and marine conditions should be identified. Applicable seismic requirements and project documentation requirements should also be provided.

Drawings, equipment schedules, structural plans, and BIM/CAD information can further improve the accuracy of the isolation selection and coordination process.
